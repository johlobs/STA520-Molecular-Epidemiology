B: So what makes MR two sample?
A: In lecture six we pretended one study measured everything, genotypes, exposure and outcome, in the same people. That's rare. The big GWAS of LDL and the big GWAS of coronary artery disease were done in different cohorts, and they only publish summary statistics.
B: So the SNP effects on the exposure come from one GWAS...
A: And the effects on the outcome from another. No individual level data needed. And you can use the largest published GWAS for each trait.
B: Then it's just MR with bigger numbers. Easier.
A: No. My unpopular opinion for today. Two sample MR is regular MR with more paperwork. The three assumptions don't go away. Relevance, independence, exclusion restriction. What changes is only where the numbers come from.
B: What do you need per SNP?
A: The rs number, the effect allele, beta and standard error for the exposure, and beta and standard error for the outcome. Plus the other allele and allele frequency, for harmonization.

B: Step four. Estimation.
A: One SNP, the Wald ratio. Outcome beta over exposure beta. Zero point zero three over zero point zero eight, zero point three seven five. The change in outcome per unit of genetically predicted exposure.
B: Several SNPs?
A: Inverse variance weighted. Each SNP's weight is its exposure beta squared, divided by the outcome standard error squared. A SNP that moves the exposure a lot and has a precise outcome estimate counts more. On the slide table, IVW gives about zero point three five.
B: Then units.
A: The slides use systolic blood pressure and pulse wave velocity. Per one millimeter of mercury, the estimate is one point zero five. Per one standard deviation, one point two. Same biology, different scale. Always state the unit.
B: And per ten millimeters, the slide says fifty percent.
A: That's the additive shortcut. If one point zero five is a ratio, effects multiply. One point zero five to the tenth is about one point six three. Sixty three percent.

# sting
B: Part two. Polygenic risk scores.
A: Same GWAS, different question. MR asks, does X cause Y? A risk score asks, who is at higher risk? MR gives one causal estimate for a population. A risk score gives one number per person.
B: And pleiotropy?
A: A threat in MR. Fine in a risk score. Any predictive signal helps.
B: How do you compute one?
A: For each person, sum over the SNPs the number of effect alleles times the effect size from the GWAS. Someone with two copies of an allele with beta minus zero point one five and one copy of an allele with plus zero point one five gets minus zero point one five. For a disease, the betas are log odds ratios, so the score is on the log odds scale.
B: What data do you need?
A: Two independent datasets. A discovery GWAS that gives the betas. And a target sample with genotypes, outcome and covariates, where the score is computed and tested. If the same people are in both, the score looks better than it is.
B: Which SNPs go in?
A: Raw GWAS output has two problems. Most betas are imprecise and the top ones inflated by winner's curse. And SNPs in LD carry the same signal. The classical fix is clumping plus thresholding. One SNP per LD region, then keep those below a p-value threshold. The alternative is shrinkage, which keeps more SNPs but pulls each beta towards zero.
B: Hold on. Weak instruments were the villain ten minutes ago. Now they're welcome?
A: In MR, every instrument must be valid on its own. In a risk score, no single SNP has to be valid. Only the sum has to predict. The slides say it directly. Remember the weak learners from statistical learning.
B: We have a second listener question. From Lina.
H: Hello. I calculated a polygenic risk score for myself as a little practice exercise. It came back at plus two standard deviations. I've now read the discussion section of three papers at midnight. Should I be worried?
B: Lina, I think we've all been there.
A: Close the papers, Lina. Not on that number alone. A risk score is relative. Plus two standard deviations puts you in roughly the top two and a half percent of scores. Turning that into an absolute risk needs a calibrated model with the baseline risk and your other risk factors.
B: How do you evaluate a score?
A: Discrimination, with the AUC, the probability that a random case gets a higher predicted risk than a random control. Calibration, do predicted risks match observed ones? And added value. Compare age, sex, smoking and blood pressure, with and without the score.
B: And the added value is usually?
A: Small. A coronary score might move the AUC from zero point seven five to zero point seven six. Highly significant, barely useful.
B: I'm going to push back. If it's barely useful, why is everyone so excited about risk scores?
A: Because barely useful on average isn't useless. At the extremes, the top few percent, risk can be several times higher. And the score is cheap, it never changes, and you can measure it at birth.
B: But you just told Lina to close the papers.
A: Not on its own. That's the point. A score is a piece of information, not a diagnosis. And it partly works through things we already measure, like LDL and blood pressure, so in a full model it adds less than you'd hope.
B: Fine. Useful at the extremes, modest on average.
A: I'll accept that summary.
B: Anything else from the reading?
A: Accuracy is capped by the trait's SNP based heritability. It drops with ancestral distance from the discovery GWAS, which is mostly European. And in the obesity example, most people with obesity still have an average or low score.
B: And the extra paper on remnant cholesterol?
A: Extra material, not required reading. But it shows the unit lesson in real data. Per millimole, remnant cholesterol looks far more harmful than LDL. Per standard deviation, they're close. Both correct. A millimole is a much bigger step for remnants than for LDL.
