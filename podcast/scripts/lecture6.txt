B: So what's the problem we're solving?
A: My unpopular opinion for today. An observational association, on its own, proves almost nothing. Studies keep finding that people with high LDL get more heart disease. Fine. But smokers might have both. Or early disease might change LDL. Confounding on one side, reverse causation on the other. The gold standard answer is a randomized trial. You flip a coin, half get the LDL lowering drug, half don't, and the coin can't be confounded by smoking. But trials are expensive, slow, sometimes unethical, and they last a few years. So the question is whether nature has already flipped the coin for us. And it has. At meiosis, each parent passes on one of their two alleles, at random. Some of us were dealt alleles that raise LDL a little. Some, alleles that lower it. If the people dealt LDL raising alleles also get more heart disease, and those alleles do nothing else relevant, the simplest explanation is that LDL itself causes heart disease.

B: And with many instruments?
A: A Wald ratio per SNP, and you combine them. The standard method is inverse variance weighted, IVW. A regression of the outcome effects on the exposure effects, through the origin, weighted by precision. In practice, a weighted average of the Wald ratios. Plot each SNP's effect on LDL against its effect on heart disease. LDLR, PCSK9, APOE, APOB, each shifting LDL by a different amount. If they line up on one line, bigger LDL effect, proportionally bigger disease effect, that's a dose response, and the causal case gets stronger.
