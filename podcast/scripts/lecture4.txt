You record the effect size, its standard error and a p-value. That's the whole method. Everything else is about doing it carefully and reading the output correctly. And the lecture starts with a lovely point about direction. DNA can plausibly cause a phenotype. The reverse is impossible. A disease cannot change the DNA you inherited at conception. That one way street is why genetics becomes so useful for causal inference later. But a genetic association is still only an association. Confounding, chance and linkage can all produce one. The direction is certain. The cause isn't.

B: Phenotype types.
A: Continuous phenotypes, like blood pressure or cholesterol, get linear regression. Binary ones, heart attack yes or no, get logistic regression. In both, you compare the phenotype across people with zero, one or two copies, with a quiet assumption that everything else, lifestyle and so on, is randomly distributed with respect to genotype. If it isn't, you have confounding. In the linear model, beta is the change in the phenotype per extra copy. If each copy raises LDL by zero point two millimoles per liter, beta is zero point two.

B: Sample size, allele frequency and how noisy the phenotype is.
A: Correct. And two things worth noting. First, genetic effects are small. The spread within each genotype group is far bigger than the difference between the group means. Most of what makes your cholesterol high has nothing to do with this one SNP. Second, additive, dominant and recessive describe the genotype phenotype relationship, not the allele. The same allele can be dominant for one trait and recessive for another. GWAS uses additive because it's the simplest default that works reasonably across a million SNPs.
B: Covariates. What goes in besides the genotype?
A: Age, sex, sometimes BMI, technical variables like genotyping batch, and genetic principal components. The principal components handle population structure. And because of this, beta depends on what you adjusted for. Compare betas across studies only after checking that.
