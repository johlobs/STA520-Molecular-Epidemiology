B: The lecture is built around a real study.
A: MicroRNA profiling of small intestinal neuroendocrine tumours. They're often diagnosed only after they've spread, and survival is hard to predict. Biologically they're odd. Very few mutations and no classic driver mutations. They seem to be driven by large chromosomal changes and epigenetics instead.
B: Why microRNA?
A: Recap from lecture three. MicroRNAs are short regulators that bind many messenger RNAs imperfectly. One microRNA can regulate hundreds of genes. And altered microRNA expression shows up in virtually every tumour type.
B: What was the design?
A: Forty two tumours from thirty seven patients, plus six samples of normal intestinal tissue. Forty eight samples in total. The RNA was labelled with a fluorescent dye and hybridized to an array with nine hundred fifty five probes, each spotted four to eight times. A scanner reads the fluorescence, which is a relative measure of how much of each microRNA is there.
B: First exam topic. Observed variation.
A: Observed signal equals biological signal plus technical noise. The technical part comes from RNA extraction, labelling, hybridization, the scanner, and batch effects.
B: So you have to clean the data before you compare anything.
A: My unpopular opinion for today. Raw omics data is mostly a recording of what happened in the lab, with a little biology hidden inside.
B: That's a bit cynical.
A: Slightly exaggerated, but it's why preprocessing is exam material. Before trusting any biological comparison, remove as much technical noise as possible.
# sting
B: What are the preprocessing steps?
A: Four, in order. Quality control. Background correction within each array. Normalization between arrays. And averaging the replicate spots for each microRNA.
B: Background correction first.
A: The method is called normexp. It models the observed intensity as a true signal plus background noise. The noise is assumed normal and the signal exponential. Then it estimates the expected true signal given what you observed. The advantage over just subtracting the background is that you always get positive values, and low intensity measurements become more reliable.
B: And quantile normalization?
A: Three steps. Within each sample, rank all the values from lowest to highest. For each rank position, compute the mean across all samples. Then replace each value with the mean for its rank.
B: What does that achieve?
A: Every array ends up with exactly the same set of values, the same distribution. But each array keeps its own order. The highest microRNA in a sample stays the highest.
B: So after normalization, a microRNA has the same value on every array?
A: No, that's the trap. Every array has the same set of values, but a given microRNA can sit at different ranks in different arrays, so it gets different values. And the assumption behind the method is that the overall distribution should be similar across arrays. So any big difference in shape is treated as technical, not biological.
# sting
B: Next, supervised versus unsupervised.
A: Supervised asks which microRNAs differ between groups you defined in advance, like tumour versus normal. That's differential expression testing. Unsupervised asks whether samples form groups by themselves, without labels. Here, hierarchical clustering.
# sting
B: Now the big one. Why does the ordinary t-test fail?
A: The t-statistic divides the mean difference by a standard error, and the variance in it is estimated separately for every microRNA. With three versus three samples, that's only four degrees of freedom. Very typical for omics.
B: And with four degrees of freedom?
A: Three problems compound. The variance estimate is very noisy. Some features get a tiny variance purely by chance, and since it sits in the denominator, t explodes even when the difference is small. That's a false positive. And other features get a randomly large variance, so real effects are missed. Lost power.
B: So if a microRNA has a huge t-value but a small mean difference?
A: Suspect a tiny variance by luck, not a big biological effect.
B: And the solution?
A: The moderated t-statistic, from the R package limma. Instead of trusting each feature's own noisy variance, limma uses empirical Bayes to share information across all features. Each variance is shrunk towards a common variance estimated from the whole dataset.
B: In words, how does the shrinkage work?
A: The moderated variance is a weighted average of the common variance and the feature's own variance. The common one is weighted by prior degrees of freedom, the own one by the feature's residual degrees of freedom.
B: And how does it behave with more samples?
A: That's also marked exam. With few replicates, the common prior dominates, and each variance is pulled strongly towards it. That prevents false positives from randomly tiny variances. With many replicates, the feature's own data dominate, and the moderated variance converges to the ordinary sample variance.
B: We have a listener question here. It's from Viktor, who has a lab meeting tomorrow.
C: Hi. My supervisor asked why I used limma instead of a normal t-test, and I said "because the tutorial did". That didn't go well. What should I have said?
B: Viktor, we've all said that at least once.
A: Here's the model answer, Viktor. It comes in three parts, and it's marked exam.
A: One, the challenge. Omics measures tens of thousands of features on few replicates. Two, the failure of standard t-tests. Variances estimated from few replicates are unstable. Randomly low ones inflate t and give false positives. Randomly high ones mask real effects. And since this sits under every p-value, it also undermines the multiple testing correction. Three, the solution. Sharing information across the dataset, variance shrinkage or empirical Bayes moderation, stabilizes the variances. That reduces false positives, recovers power, and makes results after correction more reliable.
B: Next, log two fold change.
A: The log two transformation makes expression values more symmetric and reduces the influence of very large values. The log two fold change gives the size and direction of a difference. I'll say a value, you say what it means.

B: Now multiple testing, with the study's numbers.
A: Nine hundred fifty five tests. If nothing truly differed, you'd still expect nine hundred fifty five times zero point zero five, about forty eight, significant hits at p below zero point zero five.
B: Forty eight discoveries out of nothing.
A: Each test has a five percent false positive risk from noise alone. Run hundreds, and false hits become nearly guaranteed. The chance of at least one false positive is essentially one. So the slide says it directly. Because this is multiple testing, the significance cutoff itself must change.
B: Then the two error rates. Family wise error rate and false discovery rate.
A: Expect to define, distinguish and calculate both. The family wise error rate is the probability of at least one false positive among all tests. Even one false lead counts as failure of the whole experiment. Very strict. Classic method, Bonferroni.
B: And the false discovery rate?
A: The expected proportion of false positives among the hits you call. It accepts a known fraction of wrong hits and controls that fraction. Classic method, Benjamini Hochberg.
B: So if a screen calls two hundred hits with FDR at five percent?
A: About ten of them are expected to be false. Not "at most five percent chance of any false hit". That would be family wise control.
B: And a listener question that fits right here. From Elin.
H: Hello. I've written FDR and FWER on so many flashcards that they now look like the same word. Is there a simple way to keep them apart?
A: Elin, think of it as one versus a fraction. Family wise error rate worries about even one false positive. False discovery rate accepts a fraction of false positives and controls how big that fraction is.
# sting
B: Bonferroni first.
A: Reject if the p-value is at most alpha divided by the number of tests. Or equivalently, multiply each p-value by the number of tests, cap it at one, and compare with alpha.
B: Its properties?
A: Valid for any number of tests, any test type, regardless of dependence between tests. Always safe. It controls the strictest bar. And for this study, zero point zero five divided by nine hundred fifty five is about zero point zero zero zero zero five. Many true effects won't survive that. Power collapses.
# sting
B: Now Benjamini Hochberg. The exam calculation.
A: Three steps, reproduce them exactly. One. Sort the p-values from smallest to largest. Two. Find the largest rank k where the p-value is at most k over m times Q. Here m is the number of tests and Q is the accepted false discovery rate, often zero point zero five. Three. Call every test with a p-value up to that one significant.
B: How is that different from Bonferroni?
A: Bonferroni has one fixed threshold. The Benjamini Hochberg threshold rises with rank. So larger p-values can still be significant if they clear the higher bar for their rank. That's why it finds more true positives.
B: Walk me through the instructor's example.
A: Eight tests, Q zero point zero five. The thresholds go up in steps of zero point zero zero six two five. Ranks one to four pass. The fourth p-value is zero point zero two four, against a threshold of zero point zero two five. Ranks five to eight fail. So k is four, and tests one to four are significant.
B: And Bonferroni on the same data?
A: Zero point zero five divided by eight is zero point zero zero six two five. Only test one passes.
B: You said largest k. Why does that matter?
A: This is the trap that catches people every year. You don't stop at the first p-value that fails. You look for the largest rank that passes. If rank five failed but rank six passed, then all ranks one to six are significant, including rank five.
A: Your turn. Five sorted p-values. Zero point zero zero four, zero point zero two six, zero point zero three, zero point zero four five and zero point three. # normal
B: And adjusted p-values?
A: Same decision, different format. For each sorted p-value, compute p times m over its rank. Then go from the bottom of the list upwards, and take the running minimum. Each adjusted value is the smaller of its own value and the adjusted value below it. That makes the adjusted p-values never decrease down the list. In R, it's p dot adjust with method B H.
B: Honestly, I'd still rather use Bonferroni. It's always safe. Nobody can accuse you of false positives.
A: Safe, and almost useless in omics.
B: Useless is harsh.
A: Zero point zero five divided by nine hundred fifty five. Most real effects never get there. You'd be safe and empty handed.
B: Fine. Then why exactly does the false discovery rate fit omics better?
A: Four reasons, a likely essay question. One, too many tests for Bonferroni. Its threshold heads towards zero. Two, omics screens are exploratory. The goal is a shortlist of candidates, and a controlled fraction of false leads is an acceptable price. Three, findings are validated afterwards with another method or cohort. And four, features are correlated, so the effective number of independent tests is smaller than m. Bonferroni ignores that and stays needlessly strict.
B: And the study did validate its findings?
A: Yes. Micro RNA three seventy five was confirmed with other lab methods in a separate cohort. In the paper, all p-values were adjusted with Benjamini Hochberg. Tumour versus normal gave one hundred fifty five significant microRNAs.
# sting
B: Then clustering.
A: Unsupervised hierarchical clustering of the microRNA profiles. Samples separated into normal, primary and metastatic groups, and the metastases split further into two clusters, M one and M two. M one had higher grade, more proliferation, and gain of chromosome fourteen. And M one patients had shorter survival.
B: And they compared microRNA expression between M one and M two.
A: If you build groups from the data and then test the same data between those groups, you're grading your own exam.
B: Circular reasoning.
A: Exactly, and it's marked exam. The clusters were created from microRNA expression. Then microRNA expression was tested again between those same clusters. Any clustering produces groups that differ in the variables used to build it, almost by construction. So the significance is inflated.
B: What's the general rule?
A: Don't use one dataset to both discover a grouping and test differences across it, without independent validation or a correction for the selection.
B: And the survival analysis?
A: One Cox regression per microRNA, adjusted for age and gender. Micro RNA three seventy five came out on top. Higher expression, longer survival.

B: Let's wrap up with the lecture's own take home messages.
A: One. High dimensionality leads to noisy measurements leads to multiple testing. Two. Observed variation is biology plus technical noise, so preprocess, with quantile normalization as the worked example. Three. The ordinary t-test fails with small samples, and the moderated t is the solution.
B: And the rest?
A: Four. Family wise error rate versus false discovery rate, and be able to calculate Bonferroni and Benjamini Hochberg by hand. Five. Validate screening hits in an independent cohort with an independent method. And as a bonus, never test clusters with the data that built them.
