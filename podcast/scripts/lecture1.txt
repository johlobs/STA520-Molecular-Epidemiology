# title: Lecture 1 — The biological language of molecular epidemiology
# Hosts: A = Maya (af_heart), B = Sam (am_michael). One line per turn.
A: Welcome to the S T A five twenty study podcast. I'm Maya.
B: And I'm Sam. This is episode one, the biological language of molecular epidemiology.
A: Let's start with the big picture. Sam, what is molecular epidemiology, in one sentence?
B: It's epidemiology that looks inside the body. Classical epidemiology links an exposure, like smoking or a drug, to a disease, and treats everything in between as a black box. Molecular epidemiology opens that box.
A: So instead of only asking "does smoking go with lung cancer", you measure things in between.
B: Exactly. DNA variants, RNA levels, proteins, metabolites. You measure them across many people, and you ask how they relate to exposures and to disease.
A: And this is not the same thing as biology.
B: No, and the course makes a point of that. A molecular epidemiologist works with statistical evidence at the population level. Associations, effect sizes, p-values. A molecular biologist gives the mechanistic answer, how the change actually works inside the cell.
A: So statistics points to candidates, and the lab confirms or explains the mechanism.
B: Right. The two fields need each other, and they need a shared vocabulary. That's really what this first lecture is about.
A: There's one question I'd call the central question of the whole course.
B: Go on.
A: When a molecule is statistically associated with a disease, is that causal? Or is the molecule just a consequence of the disease, or confounded by something else?
B: Yes. A molecule linked to a disease can play three roles. It can cause the disease. It can reflect a disease process that is already running. Or it can simply be associated through some third factor. Most of the course is about telling those three apart, and lecture six, on Mendelian randomization, attacks it head on.
A: Let's get to the running example, because it comes back again and again.
B: The heart attack family. A young patient has a myocardial infarction, a heart attack, much earlier than age would explain. The father also had an early heart attack. That suggests something hereditary.
A: And when you test the family's blood lipids?
B: The patient and several first degree relatives all have very high L D L cholesterol. So two conclusions. Disease can be inherited, and high L D L cholesterol seems linked to early coronary heart disease.
A: Quick background on L D L, since it's everywhere in this course.
B: L D L stands for low density lipoprotein. These are particles of protein and lipid that carry cholesterol in the blood. The liver clears most of them, mainly through the L D L receptor on liver cells. The more receptors on the surface, the faster L D L is removed from the blood.
A: And if L D L stays high, more of it gets into the artery wall, gets trapped, and plaque starts to build up. That's atherosclerosis.
B: So in the family, the molecular answer turns out to be a mutation in the gene for the L D L receptor. The receptor binds L D L less well, liver cells clear it more slowly, and blood levels rise.
A: But the lecture warns against overselling this.
B: It does. Single genes with strong effects like this are uncommon causes of heart disease in the population. Most cardiovascular disease comes from many small effect variants plus lifestyle and environment. Still, finding the mechanism is valuable, because it points to a drug target.
A: Which brings us to P C S K nine.
B: P C S K nine is a protein that promotes degradation of the L D L receptor. It breaks receptors down.
A: So if you want more receptors, you could try to make the body produce more of them.
B: But drugs that increase production of a protein are hard to make. Drugs that block a protein, or reduce how much of it is made, are much easier. So instead you target P C S K nine. Block it, fewer receptors get broken down, more receptors stay on the liver cells, and L D L cholesterol goes down.
A: Let me say the chain out loud. P C S K nine breaks down L D L receptors. Receptors clear L D L from the blood. L D L builds up in artery walls. That narrows the coronary arteries and leads to coronary heart disease.
B: And the drug acts at the very top, on P C S K nine. Note that this pathway is mainly a running example. Keep it in your head, because it shows up in lectures two, three, six and eight. Gene regulation, R N A drugs and antibodies, Mendelian randomization, and protein M R.
A: Now a part that was on the professor's list. Molecular measurement layers.
B: Five layers. First, D N A and genetic variation. It tells you inherited predisposition, and it's stable. It doesn't change over your lifetime.
A: Second, R N A. Which genes are being expressed right now. It's dynamic, and it depends on the cell and the tissue.
B: Third, proteins. The functional molecules and the signaling. They reflect abundance and activity.
A: Fourth, metabolites. Small molecules like glucose. They reflect the ongoing biochemical state, and they change fast.
B: And fifth, cells and cell markers. Cellular composition and state, which depends on context.
A: The contrast to remember is fixed D N A versus dynamic R N A and protein. Lecture eight leans on that.
B: Then there's the causal chain the course uses. Genetic variation, to gene expression, to protein amount or function, to metabolites and cellular processes, to cell behavior, to tissue and organ function, to disease.
A: With two caveats.
B: First, it's not always one directional. Disease itself can change R N A, protein and metabolite levels. So a molecular change you observe might be a consequence of disease.
A: And second, every level can be a cause, an intermediate, or a consequence, depending on the case. That ambiguity is exactly why Mendelian randomization is needed later.
B: Next, biomarkers. A biomarker is any measurable biological characteristic that gives information about a biological process, an exposure, a disease, or a treatment response.
A: And being a biomarker does not mean being causal. That's the trap.
B: The lecture gives five roles. An exposure marker, like cotinine for tobacco. A marker of an ongoing process, like C R P for inflammation. A marker of disease or tissue injury, like troponin for heart muscle damage. A marker of future risk, like L D L cholesterol. And a marker of treatment response, like the drop in L D L after starting a statin.
A: Troponin is the one to remember. It rises sharply after a heart attack because damaged heart cells release it. It's a great predictor, but lowering troponin would not treat anything.
B: Useful predictor, not a cause. That example comes back in lecture eight.
A: Let's do the basic biology, which is all on the professor's list. The central dogma.
B: D N A to R N A to protein. D N A is the durable information store. R N A is a temporary working copy of a gene. Proteins are the functional molecules made from that copy. The two steps together, transcription and translation, are called gene expression.
A: Proteins are chains of amino acids. There are twenty standard amino acids, and a typical human protein is a few hundred amino acids long. The median is around three hundred seventy five.
B: Short chains, roughly two to fifty amino acids, are called peptides.
A: The lecture lists six things proteins do. Sam, want to run through them?
B: Catalyzing reactions, that's enzymes. Synthesizing and repairing D N A. Transporting molecules, like hemoglobin carrying oxygen. Sending and receiving signals. Responding to stimuli, like antibodies. And structural support, like collagen.
A: For signaling, keep the signal and the receptor apart. Insulin is the signal. The insulin receptor is the detector. And the L D L receptor is a receiver.
B: Then the four levels of protein structure. Primary is the linear sequence of amino acids. Secondary is local folding, helices and sheets. Tertiary is the full three dimensional fold of one chain. And quaternary is several folded chains assembled into one complex.
A: And not every protein has a quaternary level.
B: Why does this matter for genetics? The amino acid order determines the fold, and the fold determines the function. Change one amino acid and you can change the function. That's the direct link from a D N A mutation to a changed protein to a changed phenotype.
A: With one side note. Drugs can bind a protein and change what it does without touching the D N A at all. That's how most medicines work, as opposed to gene therapy.
B: Now D N A itself. Genes are made of D N A, which is a chain of nucleotides. Four of them. A, adenine. C, cytosine. G, guanine. T, thymine.
A: Two strands wind into a double helix, held together by weak hydrogen bonds. Weak on purpose, so the strands can separate for copying and for reading genes.
B: The information is in the order of the letters. With ten nucleotides there are four to the power ten possible sequences, about a million.
A: And a teaser for lecture two. How many letters do you need to code for one amino acid?
B: Two letters only give sixteen combinations, too few for twenty amino acids. Three letters give sixty four. So three letters, a codon, specify one amino acid.
A: And copying works through complementary base pairing. A pairs with T, C pairs with G. Each strand is a template for a new one.
B: Quick check. If one strand reads A T G C, what sits opposite?
A: T A C G. And it runs in the opposite direction, because the strands are antiparallel.
B: Good. Then chromosomes. Humans have twenty three different chromosomes, and most cells have forty six, in twenty three pairs. One of each pair from the mother, one from the father.
A: And the classic comparison, mitosis versus meiosis.
B: Mitosis gives two identical cells with the full forty six chromosomes. That's ordinary growth and repair. Meiosis gives four different cells, sex cells, each with twenty three. Fertilization then restores forty six.
A: And the four meiosis cells differ because of recombination, which shuffles the parents' variants. That's lecture three, and it's why siblings differ.
B: Last, lipids. Molecules that dissolve poorly in water. Fats, phospholipids, cholesterol and steroid hormones. Phospholipids and cholesterol build cell membranes.
A: Which ties back to where we started. The L D L story is really a story about transporting lipids.
B: Let's wrap up with the three things to take away.
A: One. A molecule linked to disease can be a cause, a consequence, or just associated. The course is about telling these apart.
B: Two. The P C S K nine, L D L receptor, L D L cholesterol pathway. Know it well, it keeps coming back.
A: Three. The five measurement layers, with stable D N A versus dynamic R N A and protein, plus the basic biology. The central dogma, protein structure, base pairing, and mitosis versus meiosis.
B: Next episode, lecture two. How genes are actually read and regulated. Thanks for listening.
A: See you there.
