B: Small RNAs first. Last time we had messenger, ribosomal and transfer RNA. What's new?
A: Non coding RNA genes. They're transcribed into RNA but never translated. The RNA itself is the product. Small nuclear RNA handles splicing, cutting non coding pieces out of a pre messenger RNA. And microRNA regulates gene expression. Then there's small interfering RNA, and microRNA versus small interfering RNA is a classic comparison. Both are about twenty two nucleotides long. MicroRNA is encoded in your own genome. Small interfering RNA comes from double stranded RNA. But the key difference is how they bind. MicroRNA binds its targets imperfectly, so one microRNA can regulate many genes at once, by blocking translation or speeding up degradation. Small interfering RNA binds almost perfectly, to one target, and has it cut and destroyed.

B: Germline versus somatic. A typical definition question.
A: Germline mutations are in egg or sperm cells. So they're in every cell of the body, they can be passed on to children, and you find them with a routine blood or saliva test. They cause inherited disorders and hereditary cancer syndromes. Somatic mutations are acquired during life, in non reproductive cells. They're confined to the affected tissue or tumor, they cannot be passed on, and you need a sample of the diseased tissue to find them. So if a mutation is in a tumor biopsy but not in the patient's blood, it's somatic. A germline mutation would be everywhere, including blood. The lecture's worked example is acute myeloid leukemia. Not a solid tumor, but it follows the somatic pattern. The diagnostics work in four steps. Find mutations specific to the leukemic cells. Design PCR primers around a candidate. Sequence that small region across many cells, which gives very high read depth. And ask, is the mutation there, and at what variant allele frequency?

B: Mendel's three laws.
A: One, dominance. In a heterozygote, the dominant allele decides the phenotype. Two, segregation. You carry two alleles per gene, but each gamete gets only one. Three, independent assortment. Alleles of different genes are inherited independently. Except when the genes sit close together on the same chromosome. My unpopular opinion for today. Mendel was lucky with his peas. Most of his traits happened to behave. Biology is rarely that polite.
B: That's linkage.
A: During meiosis, homologous chromosomes swap segments. That's recombination. Genes close together are rarely separated, because a crossover rarely lands in the short stretch between them. The recombination frequency goes from zero, complete linkage, to fifty percent, which means unlinked. It stops at fifty because fifty percent is what pure chance gives you. Recombination can push linked genes towards independence, never past it.
B: Then linkage disequilibrium. Honestly, isn't that just linkage with a longer name?
A: No, and I'll die on this hill.
B: Here we go.
A: Linkage is about families. Genes close together travel together from parent to child. Linkage disequilibrium is about populations. It's the non random association of alleles at different loci, across people.
B: Alleles traveling together. Same thing.
A: Same spirit, different level. Family versus population. And you measure it differently. Compare the observed frequency of a haplotype with what you'd expect if the loci were independent. Allele [A](/ˈeɪ/) has frequency zero point five, allele B zero point four. Independent means the [A B](/ˈeɪ bˈiː/) haplotype should have frequency zero point two. If you observe zero point three, they're in linkage disequilibrium, and the difference, zero point one, is the coefficient D.

B: A first look at GWAS.
A: A genome wide association study scans millions of SNPs and asks, for each one, is one allele more common among people with the trait? And here's the uncomfortable truth. The top hit is often innocent. It's standing next to the guilty one. Nearby variants are inherited together, so a significant SNP may just tag the real causal variant. The association is borrowed. The causal variant might not even be on the array.
B: And the arrays work how?
A: Short DNA probes, each recognizing one allele. Fragment the person's DNA, let it bind, read the fluorescence. If both the [A](/ˈeɪ/) signal and the G signal light up, the person is a heterozygote.
