B: What does the data look like before anything is run?
A: One row per person. An ID, the phenotype, the SNP dosage, zero, one or two, and covariates. For a continuous phenotype you fit a linear model with dosage as X and the phenotype as Y. For a binary one, logistic. Adjusted for three kinds of things. Technical variables like batch or machine. Biological covariates like age, age squared and sex. And population structure, often the first ten principal components. Exactly the model from lecture four, now as what you actually run. Then the pipeline. People come to study visits, blood is drawn, DNA extracted. You end up with three kinds of data, all linked by subject ID. Phenotypes and covariates as text files, and genotypes as a big binary file with millions of SNPs per person. Those go into PLINK, together with quality control and principal components computed beforehand. Out comes summary data. One row per SNP instead of one row per person. Then the threshold, the Manhattan and QQ plots, significant SNPs grouped into loci, and a lead SNP for each locus.

B: Then back to the neighbors. Is LD just about distance?
A: That's the intuitive version, and it's wrong often enough to matter. LD is usually strongest locally. But it differs between populations, because of different recombination histories and drift. And it depends on both SNPs' allele frequencies. A common SNP and a rare SNP can sit side by side and still have a tiny r squared, simply because their frequencies don't allow a high correlation.
