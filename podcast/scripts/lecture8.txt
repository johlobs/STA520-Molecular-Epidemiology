B: Why proteins? We already have DNA.
A: DNA is fixed for life. Proteins change with diet, disease, drugs and age, and they do the actual work. They carry out functions, send signals, and most drugs act on them. And one gene doesn't mean one protein. Insulin is cut out of a larger precursor, proinsulin. Alternative splicing, cleavage, chemical modifications after translation like phosphorylation and glycosylation, where the protein ends up, complexes. The proteome is more complex than the genome, and far more dynamic. And a warning that will matter later. Protein amount is not protein function. A mutation can leave the concentration perfectly normal while the protein works completely differently. Most methods measure amount. In plasma, the level is a net balance of production, release, distribution, degradation, uptake and clearance. A change in plasma level can come from any of those. Troponin is the example. Normally very low. After a heart attack, damaged heart muscle releases it. Not secreted on purpose. It signals damage.

B: How do you actually measure proteins?
A: Three families. Antibody methods, like ELISA and Western blot. An antibody binds its target, signal proportional to amount. Targeted, a few proteins, quantitative with standards. The basis of clinical chemistry. Mass spectrometry. Proteins are cut into peptides by trypsin, which cuts after lysine and arginine, separated by liquid chromatography, and weighed. It can discover forms nobody looked for, isoforms included. Technically complex. And high throughput affinity platforms, Olink and SomaScan. Thousands of proteins per sample, built for large cohorts. UK Biobank measured about three thousand proteins in about fifty thousand people. The catch is relative abundance, not absolute concentration.
B: So if I want an isoform nobody has described?
A: Mass spectrometry. The others only see what they were built to look for.

A: A GWAS with a protein instead of a SNP. Regress the phenotype on the protein, adjusted for age, sex and so on. Standardized protein, beta per standard deviation. Or turn it around, with the protein as the outcome. Three thousand proteins at p below zero point zero five is about a hundred fifty false positives, so you control the false discovery rate, usually Benjamini Hochberg. Unlike GWAS, no single conventional threshold.
