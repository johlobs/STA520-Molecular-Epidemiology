Martin’s key concepts

The 20 concepts on the key-concepts list for GWAS, Mendelian randomization and proteomics, one module each

On 2 October the course published Martin’s key concepts, a list of 20 concepts for the GWAS, Mendelian randomization (MR) and proteomics part of the course (Lectures 4–6, 8 and 9). When the lecture pages were written this list did not exist, so the material is spread across many sections and some concepts only got a sentence or two.

This page collects the list into 20 short modules, in the list’s own order. Each module gives an exam-ready definition you can say in one or two sentences, the common confusion to avoid, a quick check, and links to the lecture sections that go deeper. Three concepts that were thin in the lectures get more space here: additive, dominant and recessive models (K.4), colocalization (K.19) and biomarker vs causal protein (K.20).

Part 1: From SNP to GWAS

K.1 SNP (single-nucleotide polymorphism)

TipSay it in one sentence

A SNP is a single base position in the genome where people in a population carry different nucleotides (alleles), for example some have C and others T; each SNP has an identifier such as an rs number from dbSNP.

  • Usually biallelic: two alleles, often called the reference/other and the effect allele in GWAS files.
  • “Polymorphism” traditionally means common enough to be a normal variant in the population (often minor allele frequency ≥ 1%). A rare change in one family is usually called a variant or mutation.
  • SNPs are what SNP arrays genotype and what GWAS tests, one at a time.

Don’t confuse: a SNP is a position with variation in the population; an allele is one of the possible bases at that position; a genotype is the pair of alleles one person carries.

Deeper: 3.3c dbSNP and rs numbers · 4.2 SNPs, alleles, MAF

A deletion of one gene that only some people carry
A single base position where alleles differ between people
A mutation that arises in a tumour but not in the germline
The one allele that a person has at a given position
A SNP is a position with variation across the population. One person’s pair of alleles at it is their genotype.

K.2 Genotype and allele dosage (0/1/2)

TipSay it in one sentence

The genotype is the pair of alleles a person carries at a SNP; the allele dosage codes it as the number of copies of the effect allele, 0, 1 or 2, which is the X variable in the GWAS regression.

Genotype (effect allele = T) CC CT TT
Dosage 0 1 2
  • Imputed SNPs give an expected dosage between 0 and 2 (for example 1.7), because the genotype is predicted with uncertainty.
  • The dosage only means something once you know which allele is counted. Swap the effect allele and every dosage becomes 2 − dosage, and β changes sign.

Don’t confuse: dosage counts the effect allele, which is not always the minor allele or the “risk” allele.

Deeper: 4.4 The additive model · A3 Reading association output

2, because the person has two copies of an allele
1, because G is the other allele at this SNP
0, because the person has no copies of A
It cannot be coded without knowing the MAF
Dosage counts copies of the effect allele. GG has zero A alleles, so dosage = 0.

K.3 Minor allele frequency (MAF)

TipSay it in one sentence

The minor allele frequency is the frequency of the less common allele at a SNP in a given population, so it is always between 0 and 0.5.

  • Computed from genotype counts: each heterozygote contributes one copy, each homozygote two. With \(n_{AA}, n_{Aa}, n_{aa}\): \(f_a = (2n_{aa} + n_{Aa}) / (2N)\).
  • Low MAF means low power: few people carry the allele, so β is estimated with a large SE. Rare variants are why GWAS QC often filters on MAF.
  • MAF is population-specific. The minor allele in Europeans can be the major allele in another ancestry, which matters when combining datasets (seminar question on GLGC vs 1000 Genomes).

Don’t confuse: MAF is not the same as the effect allele frequency (EAF). If the effect allele is the major allele, EAF = 1 − MAF.

Deeper: 4.2 SNPs, alleles, MAF and genotype frequencies · 3.9 Hardy–Weinberg

0.04, the share of people who are TT
0.36, the share of people who carry a T
0.20, the share of all alleles that are T
0.80, the share of all alleles that are C
T alleles = 2 × 40 + 320 = 400 out of 2,000 alleles = 0.20. T is the less common allele, so MAF = 0.20.

K.4 Additive, dominant and recessive models

TipSay it in one sentence

The genetic model says how the genotype is coded in the regression: additive counts copies (0/1/2, each copy adds the same effect), dominant compares carriers with non-carriers (0/1/1), and recessive compares two copies with the rest (0/0/1).

Coding of the same SNP under the three models (effect allele = T)

Model CC CT TT What β means
Additive 0 1 2 Change in phenotype per extra copy of T
Dominant (T dominant) 0 1 1 Difference between carriers (one or two T) and non-carriers
Recessive (T recessive) 0 0 1 Difference between TT and everyone else

Why GWAS use the additive model (Lecture 4, “Worth noting 2”): you test a million SNPs and do not know each one’s true pattern. The additive model is the simple default; it has reasonable power even when the true pattern is dominant or recessive, and it uses one degree of freedom. Dominant and recessive models are useful when you study one specific SNP or gene in depth.

Don’t confuse: dominance describes the genotype–phenotype relationship, not the allele. The same allele can act dominant for one phenotype and recessive for another (Lecture 4).

Deeper: 4.4 The additive model · 3.6 Mendelian genetics

Each extra copy of T is compared with one fewer copy
People with at least one T against people with no T
People with two copies of T against everyone else
Heterozygotes against both kinds of homozygote
Recessive coding is 0/0/1: only TT is coded 1, so β is the difference between TT and the CC + CT group.
Dominant, since one copy has the full effect
Additive, since each copy adds one unit
Recessive, since only TT differs from CC
None, since the heterozygote should lie halfway
CT already has the same mean as TT, so one copy is enough: a dominant pattern (0/1/1). An additive pattern would give about 4.0, 4.5, 5.0 or 4, 5, 6.

K.5 Genome-wide association study (GWAS)

TipSay it in one sentence

A GWAS tests each of roughly a million SNPs across the genome, one at a time, for association with a phenotype, using a regression of the phenotype on allele dosage adjusted for covariates such as age, sex and genetic principal components.

  • Hypothesis-free: no candidate gene is chosen in advance.
  • Output: one β, SE and p per SNP (summary statistics, K.11), shown as a Manhattan plot (position vs −log₁₀ p) and checked with a QQ plot.
  • Continuous phenotype → linear regression (β = mean difference per allele); binary → logistic regression (β = log-odds per allele, OR = e^β).
  • A GWAS finds associations. The top SNP is usually a tag in LD with the causal variant, and the association can still be confounded (population structure).

Don’t confuse: GWAS finds loci, not genes or mechanisms. Going from a hit to biology needs fine-mapping, QTL data, colocalization, MR or experiments.

Deeper: 4.6 The GWAS scan · 4.9 Population structure · 4.10 From hit to biology · 5.2 The full GWAS pipeline

To increase the number of SNPs that can be tested
To adjust for ancestry differences that can confound
To convert the additive model into a dominant one
To remove SNPs that are in strong LD with each other
If allele frequencies and the phenotype both differ between ancestry groups, SNPs look associated because of ancestry. The PCs capture ancestry and remove that confounding.

K.6 Effect size, standard error and p-value

TipSay it in one sentence

The effect size β is the estimated change in the phenotype per copy of the effect allele (log-odds for a binary outcome), the standard error says how precisely β is estimated, and the p-value measures the evidence against β = 0, based on \(z = \beta / SE\).

Example. β = 0.05 SD per allele, SE = 0.008.

Step Value
z = β / SE 0.05 / 0.008 = 6.25
Two-sided p ≈ 4 × 10⁻¹⁰, below 5 × 10⁻⁸
95% CI 0.05 ± 1.96 × 0.008 = 0.034 to 0.066
  • SE shrinks with larger sample size, higher MAF and lower phenotype variance. That is why a tiny β can have a tiny p in a large GWAS.
  • Binary outcome: β is a log(OR). OR per allele = e^β; for two copies under the additive model, OR = e^(2β).
  • p measures evidence, not importance. A highly significant SNP can explain almost none of the phenotype variance.

Don’t confuse: a small p-value does not mean a large effect. Compare β (and its units) to judge size.

Deeper: 4.3 Phenotype types and the per-allele effect · 4.4 β, SE and p

SNP 2 has a larger effect on the phenotype
SNP 2 has a higher MAF or a larger sample
SNP 2 lies closer to the gene it regulates
SNP 2 was tested with the dominant model
SE depends on sample size, allele frequency and phenotype variance. With β the same, a smaller SE comes from more information: more people or more carriers of the allele.

K.7 Genome-wide significance

TipSay it in one sentence

Genome-wide significance is the conventional threshold p < 5 × 10⁻⁸ for a GWAS hit; it is a Bonferroni correction of α = 0.05 for about one million independent common-variant tests in the genome.

\[\frac{0.05}{1\,000\,000} = 5 \times 10^{-8}\]

  • “Independent” is the key word: there are many more SNPs than that, but because of LD neighbouring SNPs are correlated, so the effective number of independent tests in European-ancestry genomes is about one million.
  • It controls the family-wise error rate: the chance of even one false positive across the whole scan is about 5%.
  • SNPs with 5 × 10⁻⁸ < p < 10⁻⁵ are sometimes called “suggestive”.

Don’t confuse: passing the threshold does not make a SNP causal, and failing it does not mean “no effect”; it may mean too little power.

Deeper: 4.7 Multiple testing, Manhattan and QQ plots · 7.11 FWER vs FDR · 7.12 Bonferroni

It is the p-value of the strongest SNP in the first GWAS
0.05 divided by about a million independent tests
0.05 divided by the total number of SNPs on an array
It is the FDR level that Benjamini–Hochberg gives
Bonferroni: α / m with m ≈ 10⁶ independent tests, after accounting for LD. It is a FWER threshold, not an FDR threshold.

K.8 Linkage disequilibrium (LD; r²)

TipSay it in one sentence

Linkage disequilibrium is the non-random association between alleles at nearby SNPs in a population, so knowing one SNP’s allele tells you something about the other’s; r² is the squared correlation between the two SNPs’ allele dosages, from 0 (independent) to 1 (perfect proxy).

  • LD exists because nearby SNPs are inherited together: recombination between them is rare, so the haplotypes they sat on are preserved over generations.
  • r² = 1: one SNP perfectly predicts the other, so they give the same GWAS signal. r² ≈ 0: independent.
  • LD is population-specific. Compute it in a reference panel that matches the ancestry of the GWAS (for example the European part of 1000 Genomes).
  • LD generally decays with distance, but physical distance is not LD: two close SNPs can have low r², and LD blocks vary in size.

Don’t confuse: linkage is about co-inheritance within families (recombination frequency); LD is the correlation of alleles across a population.

Deeper: 3.7 Linkage, recombination and LD · 4.8 Lead SNP and r² · 5.4b r² bands

A similar association, since B almost copies A
No association, because only one SNP can be causal
An association in the opposite direction from A
An association only if B lies inside a gene’s exon
With r² = 0.95, B’s alleles nearly predict A’s, so B shows almost the same signal. That is why GWAS hits appear as towers of correlated SNPs.

K.9 Lead SNP and GWAS locus

TipSay it in one sentence

A GWAS locus is a genomic region with a significant association signal, usually many correlated SNPs; the lead SNP is the SNP with the smallest p-value in that locus and is used to represent it.

  • A locus is defined around the lead SNP, by a distance window (for example ± 500 kb) and/or by LD with the lead SNP.
  • The lead SNP is the best statistical tag, not necessarily the causal variant. The causal variant can be any SNP in strong LD with it, or one that was not genotyped at all.
  • One locus can contain more than one independent signal (conditional analysis or clumping can reveal a secondary signal).

Don’t confuse: “lead SNP” (strongest p in the region) ≠ “causal SNP” (the variant that changes the biology) ≠ “nearest gene” (often assumed, not proven).

Deeper: 4.8 Lead SNP and r² · 4.10 Locus, lead SNP, QTLs · 5.4 Finding the causal SNP

Because the lead SNP is always in a non-coding region
Because SNPs in strong LD with it give nearly the same p
Because the lead SNP is chosen by its position in the gene
Because causal variants never reach genome-wide significance
Many SNPs in high LD carry almost the same signal, and the smallest p among them can be partly chance or better genotyping. The causal variant may be another SNP in LD or an unmeasured one.

K.10 LD clumping and independent genetic signals

TipSay it in one sentence

LD clumping reduces a list of significant SNPs to independent signals: take the most significant SNP, remove all SNPs within a window that are in LD with it above an r² threshold, and repeat with the next most significant remaining SNP.

The algorithm

Step Action
1 Sort significant SNPs by p, smallest first
2 Take the top SNP as an index SNP
3 Remove SNPs within the window (for example 1,000 kb) with r² above the threshold (for example 0.01–0.1)
4 Repeat with the next remaining SNP until none are left
  • Why: correlated SNPs carry the same information. For MR you need independent instruments, otherwise the same signal is counted several times and the SE is too small. For PRS clumping avoids double-counting.
  • Choices matter: a wider window compares more SNP pairs (slower, catches long-range LD, leaves fewer SNPs); a lower r² threshold removes more SNPs (stricter independence).
  • More SNPs can survive clumping than there are causal variants: two weakly correlated tags of one causal variant can both pass a lenient threshold, and SNPs outside the window are never compared.

Don’t confuse: “independent” (low r² between the kept SNPs) is not “causal”. Each clump still represents a region.

Deeper: 5.4b r² bands and LD clumping · A5 Manual LD clumping · Seminar S.1 and S.5

Clumping makes every instrument genome-wide significant
Correlated SNPs would count one signal several times
Clumping removes the need to harmonise the alleles
IVW can only handle one SNP per chromosome at most
IVW treats each SNP as independent evidence. SNPs in LD repeat the same signal, so the estimate gets falsely precise.

K.11 GWAS summary statistics

TipSay it in one sentence

GWAS summary statistics are the per-SNP results of a GWAS (SNP ID, chromosome, position, effect and other allele, allele frequency, β, SE, p and N) with no individual-level data, which is why they can be shared publicly and reused, for example in two-sample MR or PRS.

  • Before using them, check four things: which allele is the effect allele, whether β is OR or log(OR), the scale of the phenotype (SD units? log?), and the genome build (hg19/GRCh37 vs hg38/GRCh38).
  • LD cannot be computed from summary statistics: they have no genotypes per person. Use a reference panel with matching ancestry.
  • Two-sample MR needs two sets: one for the exposure, one for the outcome, matched on SNP and allele.

Don’t confuse: summary statistics are per SNP, not per person. You cannot rerun the regression with new covariates from them.

Deeper: 4.6 Summary statistics · 5.5 LD with summary statistics · 9.2 The data you need

Reading off the β and p of a given SNP
Finding SNPs below p = 5 × 10⁻⁸
Estimating r² between two SNPs for clumping
Converting a log-odds β into an odds ratio
r² needs the genotypes of individuals. Summary statistics have none, so LD comes from a reference panel such as 1000 Genomes.

Part 2: Mendelian randomization

K.12 Instrumental variable (IV)

TipSay it in one sentence

An instrumental variable is a variable that shifts the exposure, shares no causes with the outcome, and affects the outcome only through the exposure; it lets you estimate a causal effect even when the exposure–outcome relationship is confounded.

  • The idea comes from econometrics. The classic example is the randomization in an RCT: the coin flip shifts treatment and is independent of everything else.
  • In MR the instrument is a genetic variant: alleles are allocated at conception, before the exposure and the disease, and independently of most lifestyle confounders.

Don’t confuse: an IV is not a confounder or a covariate to adjust for. Its job is to give variation in the exposure that confounders do not touch.

Deeper: 6.3 Genetic variants as instruments · 6.4 Why we need MR: confounding

The outcome measured at the end of follow-up
The confounders recorded at the baseline visit
The random allocation to treatment or control
The treatment the participant actually took
Allocation shifts the treatment received and is independent of confounders, just like an allele in MR. The treatment actually taken is the exposure.

K.13 Mendelian randomization (MR)

TipSay it in one sentence

Mendelian randomization uses genetic variants associated with an exposure as instrumental variables to estimate the causal effect of the exposure on an outcome, exploiting that alleles are randomly allocated at conception, which limits confounding and rules out reverse causation.

  • “Nature’s randomized trial”: people who inherited exposure-raising alleles are, on average, like people who were randomized to a slightly higher exposure for life.
  • One-sample MR: exposure and outcome in the same people. Two-sample MR: SNP–exposure and SNP–outcome effects from two different GWAS, using only summary statistics.
  • The answer is the effect per unit of genetically predicted exposure, which is lifelong and usually small; it is not the same as the effect of a drug given in middle age.

Don’t confuse: MR is only as good as its three assumptions (K.14). A significant MR estimate with a pleiotropic instrument is not causal evidence.

Deeper: 6.4 Why we need MR · 9.1 Two-sample MR · 9.7 Interpreting an MR estimate

Because the outcome GWAS is always run before the exposure GWAS
Because MR uses only SNPs located inside the exposure’s gene
Because the disease cannot change the inherited genotype
Because the Wald ratio is always positive for causal effects
The genotype is fixed at conception. Disease can change measured biomarkers but not the alleles, so the arrow cannot run from outcome to instrument.

K.14 The three MR assumptions

TipSay it in one sentence

A valid MR instrument must be (1) relevant: robustly associated with the exposure; (2) independent: not associated with confounders of the exposure–outcome relationship; and (3) satisfy the exclusion restriction: affect the outcome only through the exposure.

# Assumption How you check it Typical threat
1 Relevance p < 5 × 10⁻⁸ in the exposure GWAS; F-statistic > 10 Weak instruments
2 Independence Cannot be proven; adjust for ancestry, check associations with known confounders Population stratification, assortative mating, selection
3 Exclusion restriction Cannot be proven; biology of the gene, MR-Egger intercept, weighted median, outlier tests Horizontal pleiotropy
  • Only assumption 1 can be tested directly. Assumptions 2 and 3 are made plausible, never proven, which is why MR uses several sensitivity analyses.

Don’t confuse: “the SNP is associated with the outcome” is not an assumption. That is what MR tests, and it is only interesting if the three assumptions hold.

Deeper: 6.5 The three MR assumptions · 6.8 Sensitivity analyses · 9.3 F-statistic

Relevance, from the SNP–exposure association
Independence, from the SNP–outcome association
Exclusion restriction, from the MR-Egger slope
None of them, since all three are untestable
Relevance is visible in the exposure GWAS (p-value, F-statistic). Independence and exclusion restriction involve unmeasured paths and can only be made plausible.

K.15 Horizontal vs vertical pleiotropy

TipSay it in one sentence

Pleiotropy is one variant affecting several traits. It is vertical when the variant affects the outcome through the exposure (fine for MR), and horizontal when it affects the outcome through a separate pathway that bypasses the exposure (violates the exclusion restriction).

Causal diagrams (each arrow means “directly affects”):

  • Horizontal pleiotropy shows up as instruments whose Wald ratios disagree with the rest, an MR-Egger intercept away from zero, or IVW and weighted median giving different answers.
  • trans-pQTLs and SNPs in genes with many functions are the usual suspects. cis instruments in the exposure’s own gene reduce the risk.

Don’t confuse: vertical pleiotropy is not a problem to remove. It is the causal chain MR is designed to detect.

Deeper: 6.6 Pleiotropy · 6.8 MR-Egger and weighted median · 9.8 Pleiotropy and pitfalls

Vertical pleiotropy, which does not bias the estimate
Horizontal pleiotropy, which can bias the estimate
A weak-instrument problem under the relevance assumption
Reverse causation from stroke to blood pressure
The SNP reaches stroke through BMI, a path that does not go through blood pressure. That is horizontal pleiotropy and breaks the exclusion restriction.

K.16 Wald ratio and inverse-variance weighted (IVW) MR

TipSay it in one sentence

With one instrument the causal estimate is the Wald ratio, the SNP’s effect on the outcome divided by its effect on the exposure; with several independent instruments the IVW estimate is a precision-weighted average of the Wald ratios, equal to a weighted regression of β-outcome on β-exposure through the origin.

\[\hat\beta_{\text{Wald}} = \frac{\beta_{\text{SNP}\to Y}}{\beta_{\text{SNP}\to X}}, \qquad SE_{\text{Wald}} \approx \frac{SE_{\text{SNP}\to Y}}{|\beta_{\text{SNP}\to X}|}\]

\[\hat\beta_{\text{IVW}} = \frac{\sum_j \beta_{X_j}\beta_{Y_j}/SE_{Y_j}^2}{\sum_j \beta_{X_j}^2/SE_{Y_j}^2}\]

Example. Three clumped SNPs (exposure in mmol/L, outcome log-odds).

SNP β exposure β outcome SE outcome Wald ratio
1 0.20 0.10 0.02 0.50
2 0.10 0.06 0.02 0.60
3 0.30 0.12 0.03 0.40

IVW numerator = (0.20·0.10 + 0.10·0.06)/0.0004 + 0.30·0.12/0.0009 = 65 + 40 = 105. Denominator = (0.04 + 0.01)/0.0004 + 0.09/0.0009 = 125 + 100 = 225. IVW = 105/225 ≈ 0.47 log-odds per mmol/L, OR ≈ e^0.47 ≈ 1.6.

  • Before computing: harmonise the alleles so both β refer to the same effect allele, and for a binary outcome use log(OR).
  • Through the origin = assumes no horizontal pleiotropy (zero exposure effect → zero outcome effect). MR-Egger frees the intercept to test that.

Don’t confuse: the Wald ratio is per unit of the exposure’s scale. An exposure in SD units and one in mmol/L give different numbers for the same biology.

Deeper: 6.7 The Wald ratio and IVW · 9.5 Harmonisation · 9.6 Wald ratio and IVW

Because the exposure GWAS centres all β values at zero
Because a SNP with no exposure effect should have no outcome effect
Because weighting by precision removes the intercept term
Because it makes the estimate robust to pleiotropic SNPs
Under the exclusion restriction the only way a SNP affects the outcome is through the exposure, so zero on X must give zero on Y. A non-zero intercept (MR-Egger) suggests directional pleiotropy.

Part 3: Proteomics and genetics

K.17 Proteomics and protein abundance

TipSay it in one sentence

Proteomics is the large-scale measurement of the proteins in a sample (the proteome); most platforms measure protein abundance, the amount or concentration of each protein, often as a relative value such as Olink’s log₂-scale NPX, not its activity.

  • Why proteins: they do the work in cells, change with disease, diet, drugs and age, and are the targets of most drugs.
  • Why it is hard: plasma protein concentrations span about 10 orders of magnitude, so a few abundant proteins (albumin, immunoglobulins) dominate; one gene can give several proteoforms (isoforms, post-translational modifications).
  • Platforms: affinity-based (Olink, SomaScan: antibodies or aptamers, thousands of proteins, relative units) and mass spectrometry (peptides, can see isoforms and give absolute amounts with internal standards).
  • NPX is relative and log₂: +1 NPX = twice as much of that protein in that sample. It cannot be compared between different proteins.

Don’t confuse: abundance ≠ function. A variant can change what a protein does without changing how much there is, and an affinity assay can miss a form it does not bind.

Deeper: 8.1 Why study the proteome · 8.4 Three technology families · 8.5 Units and NPX · 8.13 Reading: comparing eight platforms

Sample B has about four times as much protein X
Sample B has 2 units/mL more protein X than sample A
Sample B has more protein X than any protein in sample A
Sample B’s protein X is more biologically active
NPX is log₂, so a difference of 2 = 2² = 4-fold for the same protein. It says nothing about other proteins or about activity.

K.18 pQTLs: cis-pQTL and trans-pQTL

TipSay it in one sentence

A pQTL (protein quantitative trait locus) is a genetic variant associated with the abundance of a protein; it is cis when it lies near the gene that encodes the protein (commonly within 500 kb–1 Mb) and trans when it lies far away or on another chromosome.

cis-pQTL trans-pQTL
Location Near the protein’s own gene Far away (> 500 kb) or another chromosome
Likely mechanism Direct: transcription, splicing, stability, secretion of that protein Indirect: transcription factors, receptors, pathways
As MR instrument Preferred: clear biology, less pleiotropy Higher risk of horizontal pleiotropy
  • Found with a GWAS that has the protein level as the phenotype: protein = β₀ + β₁ × dosage + ε.
  • Caution: a coding (missense) cis variant can change the epitope an antibody or aptamer binds and fake a pQTL: lower measured signal, unchanged amount.

Don’t confuse: cis is defined relative to the protein’s gene, not relative to any gene. A SNP next to gene A that changes protein B is trans for B.

Deeper: 8.9 pQTLs · 8.10 Protein MR · 8.11 Challenges of protein MR

A cis-pQTL, a good candidate MR instrument
A trans-pQTL, with a high risk of pleiotropy
A cis-pQTL, which proves ABC causes disease
An eQTL, since it is near a gene’s promoter
Near the protein’s own gene → cis. cis-pQTLs are preferred instruments for protein MR, but the three MR assumptions and colocalization still need checking.

K.19 Colocalization

TipSay it in one sentence

Colocalization asks whether two association signals in the same region, for example a pQTL for a protein and a GWAS hit for a disease, are driven by the same causal variant, rather than by two different variants that happen to be close together and in LD.

The logic from the slides (Lecture 8): compare the pattern of association across the region for the protein and for the disease. If one shared variant drives both, SNPs in LD with it produce the same shape of signal for both traits: the same SNPs are strongest, and the association weakens in step as LD with the shared variant falls.

Why MR alone is not enough: if SNP A raises the protein and a different SNP B in LD with A raises disease risk through another mechanism, an MR using A will still find a “protein → disease” effect. That is LD confounding. Colocalization checks for it.

NoteBeyond the slides: the formal coloc method

The widely used Bayesian method (the R package coloc) compares five hypotheses for a region: H0 no association with either trait, H1 only trait 1, H2 only trait 2, H3 both traits but different causal variants, H4 both traits and one shared causal variant. It reports a posterior probability for each, and PP.H4 above about 0.8 is often taken as evidence of colocalization. It needs only summary statistics for both traits in the region. The slides describe the idea; this formal version is background, not course material.

Don’t confuse: “both signals are in the same locus” is not colocalization. Two causal variants 50 kb apart can sit in one locus with overlapping towers of association.

K.20 Biomarker vs causal protein

TipSay it in one sentence

A biomarker is a measurable molecule that indicates a state or risk (it can be a cause, a consequence or just a correlate of disease), whereas a causal protein is one where changing its level or activity changes disease risk, which is what you need for a drug target.

Biomarker Causal protein
Question Does it predict or reflect disease? Does changing it change disease?
Evidence Association, prediction (AUC), reproducibility MR with cis-pQTLs, colocalization, experiments, trials
Useful for Diagnosis, prognosis, risk prediction, monitoring treatment Drug targets, understanding mechanism
Example type Troponin after heart damage: excellent marker, consequence not cause A protein whose cis-pQTL predicts disease in MR and colocalizes
  • A protein associated with disease in a PWAS can be (1) a cause, (2) a consequence of disease (reverse causation), or (3) a correlate sharing a confounder (smoking, BMI, inflammation).
  • A good biomarker does not have to be causal; a causal protein is not always a good biomarker (it may vary little between people or be hard to measure).
  • The route from biomarker to causal protein (Lecture 8): association → pQTL → MR and colocalization → experiments (CRISPR, cell models, animal models).

Don’t confuse: “strongly associated” and “predictive” do not mean “causal”. Lowering a consequence-marker does not undo the disease.

Check yourself: all 20 in one go

TipExplain the chain from SNP to causal protein in eight sentences, using as many key concepts as you can.

Model answer. A SNP is a position where people carry different alleles; each person’s genotype is coded as an allele dosage of 0, 1 or 2 copies of the effect allele, and the MAF says how common the rarer allele is. A GWAS regresses the phenotype on dosage for each SNP, usually under the additive model (dominant and recessive models are used for single SNPs), giving an effect size, SE and p-value per SNP. Hits below the genome-wide significance threshold 5 × 10⁻⁸ appear as towers because of LD; the SNP with the smallest p in a locus is the lead SNP. LD clumping reduces the hits to independent signals, and the results are shared as summary statistics. Using these SNPs as instrumental variables for an exposure, Mendelian randomization estimates its causal effect if the three assumptions hold, with vertical pleiotropy allowed but horizontal pleiotropy not. The Wald ratio gives one estimate per SNP and IVW combines them. In proteomics the exposure is protein abundance, the instruments are pQTLs (preferably cis, since trans carries more pleiotropy), and colocalization checks that the protein and disease signals share one causal variant. Only then does a protein move from biomarker to candidate causal protein.
