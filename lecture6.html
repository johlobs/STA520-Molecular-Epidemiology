Lecture 6 — From GWAS to Mendelian Randomization

Imputation and proxy SNPs · Bradford Hill · instruments and the three MR assumptions · pleiotropy · Wald ratio and IVW · weighted median and MR-Egger · interpretation

Observational studies keep finding that people with high LDL cholesterol get more heart disease, but that alone cannot prove LDL causes it: smokers might have both, or early disease might change LDL. The gold standard answer is a randomized trial. Mendelian randomization (MR) uses a trial that nature already ran: at conception, each person is randomly dealt alleles that raise or lower LDL a little.

If people who inherited LDL-raising alleles also have more heart disease, and those alleles do nothing else relevant, the most plausible explanation is that LDL itself causes heart disease. Genotype cannot be changed by the disease, so reverse causation is ruled out from the start.

The whole method stands or falls on three assumptions about the genetic instrument: it must affect the exposure (relevance), it must not share causes with the outcome (independence), and it must affect the outcome only through the exposure (exclusion restriction). Most of the lecture is about checking those assumptions, especially the last one, which horizontal pleiotropy breaks.

This page covers imputation and proxy SNPs, the Bradford Hill criteria, why genes are good instruments, confounding, the three assumptions with their named threats, vertical vs horizontal pleiotropy, the Wald ratio and IVW estimate, weighted median and MR-Egger, and how to interpret an MR result. This is the lecture that ties the course together.

6.1 Imputation and proxy SNPs

The problem. You want to know whether a specific variant (rsXY) affects a phenotype, but it was not measured in your data. Or: your data has SNPs + SBP, another dataset tested the same region against stroke, but the SNP overlap is poor: your lead SNPs are missing in the other dataset and vice versa. Dead end?

Two tools solve it, both using LD structure:

Tool How it works
Proxy SNP A SNP in very strong LD (\(r^2\) close to 1) with the SNP you want. It carries essentially the same signal, so it can stand in for it
Imputation Uses LD patterns plus a large external reference panel to predict the genotype at a missing SNP as a (linear) combination of the measured SNPs, e.g. \(\text{SNP}_3 = \sum_{i \in \{3,5,6,8,11\}} a_i \cdot \text{SNP}_i\) (illustrative)

Both bridge the gap between studies (or a study and a reference panel) that did not measure exactly the same SNPs.

6.2 The Bradford Hill criteria

Classical epidemiology’s considerations for judging whether an association is likely causal (predates genetic methods). Nine criteria, likely a list-recall item:

# Criterion Short meaning
1 Strength Strong associations are less likely to be fully explained by bias
2 Consistency Repeated in different populations and studies
3 Specificity One exposure linked to one outcome
4 Temporality Exposure comes before outcome (the only strictly necessary one)
5 Biological gradient Dose–response
6 Plausibility A biologically plausible mechanism
7 Coherence Fits with what is known about the disease
8 Experiment Experimental change in exposure changes the outcome
9 Analogy Similar exposures cause similar effects

Memory aid: S-C-S-T-B-P-C-E-A, “Strong Consistent Specific Timing, Biological Plausible Coherent Experimental Analogy”.

6.3 Genetic variants as instruments

Key insight: genetic variants are natural instrumental variables. Genotype is fixed at conception, so it comes first in any causal chain: no reverse causation (adult disease cannot change the genotype you were born with).

Genetic variant (SNP) ──▶ Exposure ──▶ Outcome

To use a SNP to study the causal effect of an exposure on an outcome, three core assumptions must hold: relevance, independence, exclusion restriction (Section 6.5 gives them in full).

6.4 Why we need MR: confounding

Motivating problem. Does LDL-C cause coronary heart disease (CHD)? Observationally, high LDL-C precedes CHD, but how do you rule out:

  • confounding, e.g. smoking affects both: LDL-C ← SMOKING → CHD
  • selection bias
  • measurement error
  • reverse causation (which came first?)

The classical gold standard is the randomized controlled trial (RCT): intervene on LDL-C and see whether CHD risk changes. RCTs are expensive, slow, and not always ethical or feasible.

The genetic parallel. A naturally occurring SNP that raises or lowers LDL-C is allocated at random at conception, because meiosis randomly allocates alleles to offspring, much as an RCT randomly allocates treatment. Hence Mendelian randomization: SNP → LDL-C → CHD.

RCT Mendelian randomization
Random allocation Treatment assigned by researcher Alleles assigned at meiosis
“Intervention” Drug lowers LDL-C Allele lowers LDL-C (lifelong)
Confounding Balanced by randomization Balanced, if the independence assumption holds
Reverse causation Excluded by design Excluded: genotype fixed at conception

Why is a GWAS association with CHD not enough? It says a SNP is associated with CHD (or LDL-C), not which pathway or which gene. The SNP could act through LDL-C, or through inflammation or blood pressure (SBP). MR is designed to resolve this.

6.5 Instruments and the three MR assumptions

Selecting instruments. Choose SNPs strongly associated with the exposure (e.g. LDL-C), typically \(P < 5 \times 10^{-8}\) in the exposure GWAS, and independent of each other (low LD, e.g. \(r^2 < 0.01\), via LD clumping, Lecture 5). At this stage you do not look at the SNP → outcome relationship at all; that comes in later.

ImportantThe three MR assumptions (the most important definitional block in the course)
# Assumption Meaning Named threats
1 Relevance The variant has a robust, strong association with the exposure. Usually checked by genome-wide significance in the exposure GWAS Weak instruments bias and destabilize the estimate
2 Independence The variant is not associated with confounders of the exposure–outcome relationship Population stratification, assortative mating (non-random mate choice related to the trait), selection effects (participation depends on genotype and outcome)
3 Exclusion restriction The variant affects the outcome only through the exposure Horizontal pleiotropy: another pathway. Ask: “does this SNP affect the outcome through another pathway entirely?”

6.6 Multiple instruments, dose–response and pleiotropy

With several independent instruments, each shifting the exposure by a different amount, plot each SNP’s effect on the exposure (β-exposure, e.g. β-LDL-C) against its effect on the outcome (β-outcome, e.g. β-CHD). Example SNPs: SNP1 (LDLR), SNP2 (LDLR), SNP3 (PCSK9), SNP4 (APOE), SNP5 (APOB), all genes with known roles in LDL metabolism. A consistent dose–response line (larger effect on exposure → proportionally larger effect on outcome) strengthens confidence in causality.

Pleiotropy: one variant affecting more than one trait or pathway.

Vertical pleiotropy Horizontal pleiotropy
Path SNP affects the outcome via the exposure SNP affects the outcome through a pathway independent of the exposure
Example SNP1 → PCSK9 → LDLR availability → LDL-C → atherosclerosis → CHD; or SNP2 → APOE → binding → same pathway SNP3 → LDL-C → atherosclerosis → CHD, and SNP3 → inflammation → CHD
For MR Not a problem: this is the causal chain MR is looking for Bad: violates the exclusion restriction

6.7 The Wald ratio and IVW

One instrument: the Wald ratio

\[\hat\beta_{MR} = \frac{\beta_{\text{SNP}\to\text{outcome}}}{\beta_{\text{SNP}\to\text{exposure}}}\]

“Change in outcome per genetically predicted unit change in exposure.” If the outcome is binary, β-outcome must be on the log-odds scale. The three assumptions must still hold.

Many instruments. Compute a Wald ratio per instrument (after LD clumping), then combine. If instruments give noticeably different ratios, that disagreement is informative: horizontal pleiotropy in some instruments, or weak instruments.

Inverse-variance-weighted (IVW) MR, the standard way to combine: a linear regression of β-outcome on β-exposure across the instruments, through the origin, weighted by precision. Equivalent to a weighted average of the Wald ratios:

\[\hat\beta_{IVW} = \frac{\sum_j \beta_{X_j}\beta_{Y_j}\,/\,\sigma_{Y_j}^2}{\sum_j \beta_{X_j}^2\,/\,\sigma_{Y_j}^2}\]

where \(\beta_{X_j}\) is SNP \(j\)’s effect on the exposure, \(\beta_{Y_j}\) on the outcome, and \(\sigma_{Y_j}\) the SE of \(\beta_{Y_j}\). (The slides describe the weights as the precision of the estimates, “or a combined variance term”; the formula above is the standard first-order version.)

Outlier instruments, whose Wald ratio deviates from the rest, can arise from:

  • pleiotropy / a different biological pathway
  • measurement error
  • a weak instrument
  • population differences between the exposure and outcome studies
  • chance

Agreement strengthens confidence; strong disagreement motivates sensitivity analyses.

Worked example (illustrative numbers, not from the slides). LDL-C in mmol/L, CHD on the log-odds scale.

SNP Gene β exposure (LDL-C) β outcome (log-odds CHD) SE outcome Wald ratio
SNP1 LDLR 0.20 0.100 0.020 0.50
SNP2 LDLR 0.10 0.045 0.015 0.45
SNP3 PCSK9 0.30 0.160 0.025 0.53
SNP4 APOE 0.25 0.120 0.020 0.48
SNP5 APOB 0.15 0.180 0.030 1.20 (outlier)

IVW over all five: \(\hat\beta_{IVW} \approx 0.54\) → OR ≈ \(e^{0.54} \approx 1.71\) per 1 mmol/L genetically higher LDL-C. Without SNP5: ≈ 0.50 (OR ≈ 1.65). SNP5’s much larger ratio hints at pleiotropy. Check with the Practice code below.

If IVW, weighted median and MR-Egger all point in a similar direction, confidence in causality increases substantially. The methods have different weaknesses, which are unlikely to all bias the result the same way.

It needs only half of the instrument weight to be valid
It removes every SNP with a non-zero Egger intercept
It forces the regression line through the origin
It gives all SNPs exactly the same weight
The weighted median is consistent if at least ~50% of the weight comes from valid instruments, so a minority of invalid SNPs cannot dominate it.

6.9 Interpreting an MR result

An MR estimate is the expected change in outcome per genetically predicted change in exposure. Always check:

  1. The unit of exposure: per 1 SD? per 1 mmol/L? a transformed scale? Without it the magnitude is meaningless.
  2. Direction: \(\beta > 0\) → higher exposure increases outcome risk; \(\beta < 0\) → higher exposure decreases it.

For a binary outcome, report \(e^{\beta}\) as an OR per unit of exposure.

Why-questions

TipWhy is MR sometimes called a “natural RCT”?

Alleles are randomly allocated at meiosis, independent of lifestyle and most confounders, just as treatment is randomly allocated in a trial. Genotype also cannot be changed by the disease, so reverse causation is excluded.

TipWhy is vertical pleiotropy fine but horizontal pleiotropy not?

Vertical pleiotropy is just the causal chain through the exposure broken into biological steps, which is exactly what MR measures. Horizontal pleiotropy adds a second route to the outcome that bypasses the exposure, so part of the SNP–outcome association is wrongly credited to the exposure.

TipWhy use several MR methods on the same data?

Each method fails under different conditions: IVW if any instrument is invalid, weighted median if more than half the weight is invalid, MR-Egger if InSIDE fails (and it is imprecise). Agreement across methods is much stronger evidence than any one estimate.

TipWhy do instruments need to be independent (clumped)?

IVW treats each SNP as a separate piece of evidence. Correlated SNPs repeat the same information, which overstates precision and can let one locus dominate.
