🎯 Instructor’s example questions: Lecture 7

These come straight from “Lecture 7 - questions.pdf”, the closest thing to real exam questions in the course. Answer each one on paper first.

TipL7-Q1. Explain the relationship “observed variation = biological variation + technical variation/noise.” Name two sources of technical variation mentioned in the lecture.

Model answer. Every value measured on a high-throughput platform is the sum of the true biological signal we want and variation added by the measurement process. Differences between samples can therefore come from biology or from the technology, and we must remove as much technical variation as possible (preprocessing) before comparing groups. Sources of technical variation named in the lecture (any two): RNA extraction, labelling, hybridization, scanner/detection noise, batch effects. → 7.4

TipL7-Q2. What problem does normexp background correction solve, and why is simply subtracting the background from the signal too naive a method?

Model answer. The fluorescence at each spot contains background noise on top of the true signal, and this matters most for weakly expressed miRNAs. Simple subtraction (foreground − background) is too naive because the background estimate is itself noisy: for low-intensity spots the result is often zero or negative, which is biologically meaningless and cannot be log-transformed, and the low-intensity values become very unstable. normexp models observed intensity = true signal + background, with the signal exponentially distributed and the background normally distributed, and replaces each value by the expected true signal given the observed intensity. The corrected values are always positive, and low-intensity measurements become more reliable. → 7.5

TipL7-Q3. Describe what quantile normalization does to the distributions of expression intensities across arrays, and explain why this step is necessary before comparing samples.

Model answer. It makes the distribution of intensities identical on every array: rank the values within each array, compute the mean across arrays at each rank, and replace every value by the mean for its rank. Each array keeps its internal order (the highest miRNA stays highest), but differences in overall level and spread between arrays disappear. It is needed because arrays differ systematically for technical reasons (amount of RNA, labelling efficiency, scanner settings, batch). The assumption is that the overall distribution of expression should be similar across samples, so a whole-array shift is technical. Without normalization a miRNA could look “up” in a sample simply because that whole array was brighter. → 7.5

TipL7-Q4. In the ordinary two-sample t-statistic, explain why the standard deviation estimate s becomes unreliable with only a few samples per group (e.g. 3 vs 3), and what statistical consequences (false positives/negatives) this can lead to.

\[t = \frac{\bar x_1 - \bar x_2}{s\sqrt{1/n_1 + 1/n_2}}\]

Model answer. \(s\) is estimated separately for every miRNA from only \(n_1 + n_2 - 2 = 4\) degrees of freedom. With so few observations, the sampling variability of \(s\) is huge: a feature’s estimate can easily be far below or far above its true value just by chance. Across hundreds or thousands of features, many extreme estimates are guaranteed.

  • False positives: features with a randomly small \(s\) get a small denominator and a huge \(t\), even when the mean difference is tiny.
  • False negatives (lost power): features with a randomly large \(s\) get a small \(t\), so real differences are missed.
  • The ranking of “top hits” is driven partly by noise in \(s\) rather than by effect size, and the unstable p-values also undermine the multiple-testing correction applied afterwards. → 7.7–7.8
TipL7-Q5. What is the moderated t-statistic (limma), and how does it address the problem in Question 4? Explain “shrinking gene-specific variance toward a common/global estimate”, and what happens to the moderated variance when the number of replicates is large versus small.

Model answer. The moderated t-statistic (limma) is a t-statistic in which each feature’s variance is replaced by a moderated variance estimated with empirical Bayes, sharing information across all features:

\[\tilde s_g^2 = \frac{d_0 s_0^2 + d_g s_g^2}{d_0 + d_g}\]

“Shrinking toward a common estimate” means each gene’s own variance \(s_g^2\) is pulled toward a global variance \(s_0^2\) estimated from all genes. The result is a weighted average: weight \(d_0\) (prior degrees of freedom) on the global value and weight \(d_g\) (the gene’s own residual df) on its own estimate. Extremely small and extremely large variances are pulled toward the middle, which stabilizes the denominator of \(t\) and gives fewer false positives and more power.

  • Few replicates (small \(d_g\)): \(d_0\) dominates, and each variance is pulled strongly toward \(s_0^2\).
  • Many replicates (large \(d_g\)): the gene’s own data dominate, and \(\tilde s_g^2 \to s_g^2\), so the moderated t approaches the ordinary t. → 7.7
TipL7-Q6. A miRNA happens, purely by chance, to have an unusually small variance estimate in a small study. Why does this create a false-positive risk under the ordinary t-test, and why does moderation reduce that risk?

Model answer. The variance sits in the denominator of \(t\). A randomly tiny \(s^2\) makes \(t\) very large and the p-value very small, even if the mean difference is modest or zero, so the miRNA is called significant because of noise in \(s^2\) rather than a real effect. Moderation replaces \(s^2\) by \(\tilde s^2 = (d_0 s_0^2 + d\, s^2)/(d_0 + d)\). Because the global variance \(s_0^2\) is typical rather than tiny, \(\tilde s^2\) is pulled up toward it, the denominator grows, and \(t\) shrinks back to a realistic size. With few samples the pull is strong, which is exactly when the risk is largest. (Try the moderated t drill below.) → 7.7

TipL7-Q7. State the definitions of FWER and FDR precisely, and explain the key conceptual difference between them.

Model answer.

  • FWER (family-wise error rate) \(= P(V \ge 1)\): the probability of making at least one false positive among all \(m\) tests. Here \(V\) is the number of false positives.
  • FDR (false discovery rate) \(= E\left[V/R\right]\), with \(V/R\) taken as 0 when \(R = 0\): the expected proportion of false positives among all tests called significant, where \(R\) is the number of rejections.

Key difference: FWER treats a single false positive anywhere as failure of the whole experiment, which is very strict. FDR accepts that some hits will be false and controls the fraction of false hits among the discoveries. FWER is controlled by Bonferroni (α/m); FDR is controlled by Benjamini–Hochberg. FDR control gives more power when many tests are run. → 7.11

TipL7-Q9. Give two reasons from the lecture why FDR control is generally preferred over strict FWER control (Bonferroni) in high-throughput omics studies such as the miRNA profiling paper.

Model answer. Any two of the four:

  1. Too many tests: with m ≈ 955, Bonferroni requires p < 0.05/955 ≈ 0.00005, so most true effects are missed and power collapses.
  2. Exploratory aim: the screen produces a shortlist of candidates, so a small, controlled fraction of false leads is acceptable in exchange for finding more true effects.
  3. Validation follows: hits are re-tested with an independent method or cohort (miR-375 was confirmed by qRT-PCR and in situ hybridization in a separate cohort).
  4. Correlated features: co-regulated miRNAs make the effective number of independent tests smaller than m, so Bonferroni is needlessly strict. → 7.14
TipL7-Q10. (a) Which tests related cluster membership (M1/M2) to a categorical variable such as tumour grade, and to a numerical variable such as age? Why does each fit? (b) How do M1 and M2 differ biologically and clinically?

Given: M1 had more high-grade tumours (P = 0.0011), more Ki67 > 3% (P = 0.00023), more chromosome 14 gain (P = 0.0013) and shorter overall survival than M2 (log-rank P = 0.029).

Model answer.

(a) Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables such as grade. It tests association in a contingency table (cluster × category) and is exact, so it is valid with the small cell counts you get with a few dozen tumours, where the χ² approximation is unreliable. The t-test for numerical variables such as age. It compares the mean of a continuous variable between two groups (M1 vs M2).

(b) M1 tumours are more proliferative and more aggressive: higher grade (G2/G3) and more Ki67 > 3%, both markers of cell division. They also more often carry gain of chromosome 14, fitting the idea that siNETs are driven by chromosomal alterations rather than point mutations. Clinically, M1 patients have shorter overall survival (log-rank P = 0.029). So M1 is a molecular subtype with a worse prognosis, and M2 is a less proliferative subtype with a better one. The most upregulated miRNAs in M1 (miR-1246, miR-663a) are known oncomiRs; miR-488-3p, a reported tumour suppressor, is down. → 7.16

TipL7-Q11. Why is testing for differentially expressed miRNAs between M1 and M2 an example of circular reasoning (“using the data twice”), and what does it imply for the p-values and the list of differentially expressed miRNAs?

Model answer. M1 and M2 were created from the miRNA expression data by unsupervised clustering, which by construction puts samples with similar expression together and samples with different expression apart. Testing the same miRNA data for differences between those clusters asks a question whose answer was built in by the first step. Even pure noise, clustered into two groups, would give many “significant” miRNAs.

Implications: the p-values are not valid. They are biased toward zero because the null distribution assumed by the test does not account for the groups having been selected from the data, and the multiple-testing correction does not fix this. The list of differentially expressed miRNAs (76 up, 65 down) mainly describes what separates the clusters; it is not independent evidence of biological differences. It should be treated as descriptive or hypothesis-generating, and validated in an independent cohort (or analysed with methods that account for the selection). The associations with clinical variables (grade, Ki67, survival) are more informative, because those variables were not used to build the clusters. → 7.16b

Calculation drills

🎯 Multiple testing (Lecture 7)

Benjamini–Hochberg and Bonferroni side by side. Do several: the “largest k” rule and the running minimum for adjusted p-values are where mistakes happen.

TipBH checklist for the exam
  1. Sort the p-values from smallest to largest; write the rank \(i\) next to each.
  2. Compute the threshold \(\frac{i}{m} Q\) for every rank.
  3. Find the largest \(i\) with \(p_{(i)} \le \frac{i}{m} Q\). Call it \(k\). Do not stop at the first failure.
  4. Everything with rank \(\le k\) is significant.
  5. Adjusted p: compute \(p_{(i)} \cdot m / i\), then go from the bottom up taking the running minimum. Cap at 1.
  6. Bonferroni for comparison: one threshold \(\alpha/m\) (or \(p \times m\)), same for all.
  7. State the interpretation: BH controls the FDR (expected share of false hits among hits); Bonferroni controls the FWER (probability of any false hit).

🎯 Lecture 7: high-dimensional data

TipQ1. Explain why the ordinary t-test is problematic for omics data with few samples, and how the moderated t-statistic solves it.

Model answer. In omics data we measure thousands of features on few replicates, e.g. 3 vs 3 samples, giving only \(n_1 + n_2 - 2 = 4\) degrees of freedom per feature. Each feature’s variance \(s^2\) is estimated separately and is therefore very unstable. By chance some features get a tiny \(s^2\); since \(s^2\) is in the denominator of \(t\), they get huge t-values although the mean difference is small → false positives. Others get a randomly large \(s^2\), masking real effects → lost power. This instability affects every p-value and so also undermines the multiple-testing correction.

The moderated t (limma, empirical Bayes) shares information across all features: each variance is shrunk toward a common variance \(s_0^2\) estimated from the whole dataset, \(\tilde s^2 = (d_0 s_0^2 + d\, s^2)/(d_0 + d)\). With few replicates the prior weight \(d_0\) dominates and the shrinkage is strong; with many replicates the feature’s own variance dominates. Result: fewer false positives, more power, more reliable FDR-adjusted results. → 7.7–7.8

TipQ2. Define FWER and FDR. Which correction methods control each, and which is preferred for a miRNA microarray screen? Give reasons.

Model answer. FWER = P(at least one false positive among all tests), controlled by Bonferroni (\(p \le \alpha/m\)). FDR = the expected proportion of false positives among all tests called significant, E[FP / positives], controlled by Benjamini–Hochberg.

For omics screens FDR/BH is preferred: (1) with thousands of tests Bonferroni’s threshold is so small that most true effects are missed (0.05/955 ≈ 0.00005); (2) the analysis is exploratory, producing a shortlist where a controlled fraction of false leads is acceptable; (3) hits are validated afterwards in an independent cohort/method (miR-375 by qRT-PCR and in situ hybridization); (4) features are correlated, so the effective number of tests is smaller than \(m\) and Bonferroni is needlessly strict. → 7.11–7.14

TipQ3. The study tested 955 miRNAs. How many significant results would you expect at p < 0.05 if no miRNA truly differed? What is the Bonferroni threshold?

Model answer. \(955 \times 0.05 \approx 48\) false positives. Bonferroni: \(0.05/955 \approx 5.2 \times 10^{-5}\) (≈ 0.00005). With 955 independent null tests the chance of at least one false positive is \(1 - 0.95^{955} \approx 1\), so the cutoff itself must change. → 7.10

TipQ4. Describe the preprocessing of the microarray data. What does quantile normalization do and what does it assume?

Model answer. Observed signal = biological signal + technical noise (RNA extraction, labelling, hybridization, scanner, batch). Steps: (1) quality control; (2) background correction within each array with normexp, which models observed = signal (exponential) + background (normal) and estimates the true signal, giving positive values and better low-intensity estimates; (3) normalization between arrays with quantile normalization: rank the values in each array, average across arrays at each rank, replace each value by its rank mean, so all arrays get an identical distribution while keeping each array’s internal order; (4) average replicate probes (4–8 spots) per miRNA. Assumption of quantile normalization: the overall expression distribution should be the same in every sample, so distribution differences are technical. → 7.4–7.5

TipQ5. The authors clustered the samples on miRNA expression, found clusters M1 and M2, and then reported 141 miRNAs differentially expressed between M1 and M2. Comment critically.

Model answer. This is circular reasoning: the clusters were defined from the same miRNA data that was then tested. Clustering produces groups that differ in the clustering variables almost by construction, so significant differences are expected even in pure noise and the p-values are inflated. The differences are not independent evidence. Valid alternatives: test the cluster–clinical associations (grade, Ki67, chromosome 14 gain, which used other data), validate the clusters in an independent cohort, or use methods that account for the selection. → 7.16b

TipQ6. What does log₂FC = 1.5 mean? And why log-transform expression data at all?

Model answer. \(2^{1.5} \approx 2.83\): expression is about 2.8 times higher in the first group. log₂FC = 0 means no change, +1 twice as high, −1 half as high. Log₂ transformation makes the distribution more symmetric and reduces the influence of very large raw values; on the log scale, fold changes become differences. → 7.9

TipQ7. Five patients had both a primary tumour and a liver metastasis. Why were only their primary tumours used in the tumour vs normal comparison?

Model answer. Two samples from the same patient are not independent. Including both would double-weight those patients and violate the independence assumption of the test, biasing variance estimates and p-values. → 7.9b

📌 Lectures 4–6: GWAS and Mendelian randomization

TipQ8. State the three assumptions of Mendelian randomization and give a threat to each.

Model answer. 1. Relevance: the variant is robustly associated with the exposure (genome-wide significant). Threat: weak instruments, which bias and destabilize the estimate. 2. Independence: the variant is not associated with confounders of the exposure–outcome relationship. Threats: population stratification, assortative mating, selection effects. 3. Exclusion restriction: the variant affects the outcome only through the exposure. Threat: horizontal pleiotropy (another pathway, e.g. SNP → inflammation → CHD). → 6.5

TipQ9. Distinguish vertical and horizontal pleiotropy. Which one is a problem for MR and why?

Model answer. Vertical: the SNP affects the outcome through the exposure (SNP → PCSK9 → LDLR → LDL-C → CHD); this is the causal chain MR measures, not a problem. Horizontal: the SNP also affects the outcome through a pathway independent of the exposure (SNP → inflammation → CHD); this violates the exclusion restriction and biases the MR estimate, because part of the SNP–outcome association is wrongly attributed to the exposure. → 6.6

TipQ10. Explain the Wald ratio, IVW, weighted median and MR-Egger. When would you trust a causal conclusion?

Model answer. Wald ratio (one instrument): \(\beta_{\text{outcome}}/\beta_{\text{exposure}}\), change in outcome per genetically predicted unit of exposure (log-odds for binary outcomes). IVW: weighted regression of β-outcome on β-exposure through the origin = precision-weighted average of the Wald ratios; assumes all instruments valid. Weighted median: consistent if at least ~50% of the instrument weight is valid, so robust to a minority of bad instruments. MR-Egger: free intercept; a non-zero intercept indicates directional horizontal pleiotropy; less precise and more sensitive to weak instruments, relies on the InSIDE assumption. Trust grows when all methods point in the same direction, since their weaknesses differ. Always state the exposure unit and the direction. → 6.7–6.9

TipQ11. Why are genetic variants useful for causal inference? Compare MR with an RCT.

Model answer. Genotype is fixed at conception, so it precedes the disease: no reverse causation. Alleles are allocated randomly at meiosis, like treatment in an RCT, so they are (under the independence assumption) unrelated to confounders such as smoking. An allele that lowers LDL-C is a lifelong “natural trial” of lower LDL-C. MR is cheaper and possible where RCTs are not, but relies on the three instrument assumptions. → 6.3–6.4

TipQ12. A Manhattan plot shows a tall peak with dozens of significant SNPs. Explain why, and what “lead SNP” and “locus” mean. Is the lead SNP causal?

Model answer. Nearby SNPs are correlated (in LD) because they are inherited together, so one causal variant makes all SNPs in LD with it look associated. The locus is the region containing the associated signal; the lead SNP is the one with the smallest p-value there. It is not necessarily causal: it may be a proxy, and the causal variant may not even be on the array. Ways forward: conditional analysis, credible sets, functional annotation, protein folding models, pQTL evidence, experiments (knock-in mice). → 4.8, 5.3–5.4

TipQ13. What is population stratification and how is it handled in a GWAS?

Model answer. If allele frequencies and the phenotype both differ between ancestry groups, pooling them creates a spurious SNP–phenotype association even without any biological effect. Handling: restrict to genetically homogeneous ancestry groups; adjust for genetic principal components; use mixed models that account for relatedness (e.g. regenie); check the QQ plot and genomic inflation. → 4.9

TipQ14. Why is the genome-wide significance threshold 5×10⁻⁸? Does p < 5×10⁻⁸ mean the SNP matters?

Model answer. About 1 million independent tests: \(0.05/10^6 = 5 \times 10^{-8}\) (Bonferroni); at 0.05 you would get ~50,000 false positives. Significance means statistical robustness only. It says nothing about effect size (huge samples make tiny effects significant), causality or public-health relevance (rs11591147 has a big effect but is rare, 1.5%). → 4.7, 4.10

TipQ15. What must you always know when using GWAS summary statistics?

Model answer. Which allele is the effect allele (A1/EA); whether β is an OR or log(OR) for binary outcomes; the scale of the phenotype (log? SD units?); the genome build (GRCh37/hg19 vs GRCh38). When combining datasets also check SNP coverage, allele/strand alignment and ancestry of the LD reference panel. rsIDs help avoid mismatches. → 4.6, 5.6

TipQ16. List the Bradford Hill criteria.

Model answer. Strength, Consistency, Specificity, Temporality, Biological gradient (dose–response), Plausibility, Coherence, Experiment, Analogy. → 6.2

📌 Lectures 1–3: biology and genetics

TipQ17. Explain the LDLR–PCSK9–LDL-C pathway, and how Leqvio and Repatha lower LDL-C differently.

Model answer. The LDL receptor on hepatocytes clears LDL from the blood. PCSK9 promotes LDLR degradation, so more PCSK9 → fewer receptors → higher LDL-C → more atherosclerosis. Leqvio (inclisiran) is an siRNA linked to GalNAc, which binds ASGPR on liver cells; inside, it degrades PCSK9 mRNA, so less PCSK9 is made. Repatha/Praluent are monoclonal antibodies binding free PCSK9 protein in blood, blocking its action. Both leave more LDLR and lower LDL-C: two druggable points on one pathway. D374Y (GoF) raises LDL-C; PCSK9 LoF lowers it, as the drugs mimic. → 1.2, 3.2b, 3.3

TipQ18. Compare germline and somatic mutations.

Model answer. Germline: arise in egg/sperm, present in every cell, heritable, detected in blood/saliva, cause inherited disorders and hereditary cancer syndromes. Somatic: acquired during life in non-reproductive cells, confined to the tissue/tumour, not heritable, need a biopsy of the tissue, drive cancers and benign tumours (e.g. AML, detected by targeted deep sequencing and VAF). → 3.4

TipQ19. Read this variant: NM_174936.4(PCSK9):c.1120G>T, p.Asp374Tyr, rs137852912.

Model answer. In PCSK9 transcript NM_174936 version 4 (NM_ = mRNA reference), coding DNA position 1120 has G replaced by T. At the protein level (p.) aspartic acid 374 becomes tyrosine (D374Y): a missense, gain-of-function SNV. rs137852912 is its dbSNP identifier; its genomic position is chr1:55057454 in GRCh38 and 55523127 in GRCh37 (NC_ prefix, g. coordinates). → 3.3b–3.3c

TipQ20. Compare miRNA and siRNA.

Model answer. miRNA: ~22 nt, genome-encoded, binds targets imperfectly, inhibits translation or promotes degradation, regulates many mRNAs (why it is widely dysregulated in cancer). siRNA: ~21–23 nt, from double-stranded RNA, binds with near-perfect complementarity, cleaves one specific mRNA, used as designed drugs (Leqvio). → 3.2

TipQ21. Define linkage, recombination frequency and linkage disequilibrium. How is LD detected?

Model answer. Linkage: loci close on the same chromosome tend to be inherited together. Recombination frequency \(r\) = recombinant offspring / total, 0% (complete linkage) to 50% (unlinked). LD: non-random association of alleles at different loci in a population. Detected by comparing observed haplotype frequency with the expected product of allele frequencies (e.g. expected AB = 0.5 × 0.4 = 0.20); a difference indicates LD. Quantified with \(r^2\) (> 0.8 very strong; < 0.01 treated as independent). → 3.7

TipQ22. Give the five categories of biomarker with an example each. Is a biomarker causal?

Model answer. Exposure (cotinine for tobacco), biological process (CRP for inflammation), disease/tissue injury (troponin for myocardial injury), future risk (LDL-C for atherosclerosis), treatment response (LDL-C change on lipid-lowering therapy). Being a biomarker does not imply causality. → 1.3b

📌 Lecture 8: proteomics

TipQ23. What is a cis-pQTL vs a trans-pQTL, and why are cis-pQTLs preferred as MR instruments?

Model answer. A pQTL is a SNP associated with a protein’s abundance. cis: near the encoding gene (mechanisms: transcription, splicing, stability, secretion). trans: far away, > 500 kb or another chromosome (mechanisms: transcription factors, receptors, signalling). cis-pQTLs are preferred because the link to the protein is direct and there is less risk of horizontal pleiotropy; they still need all three MR assumptions. Protein MR: \(\beta_{MR} = \beta_{GO}/\beta_{GP}\). → 8.9–8.10

TipQ24. List challenges specific to protein MR.

Model answer. Horizontal pleiotropy (especially trans-pQTLs); assay binding artifacts (coding variants change the epitope, fake lower abundance); amount ≠ function (variants changing activity without abundance are invisible); LD creating false appearance of causality (different SNPs in LD for protein and disease), checked by colocalization; one pQTL affecting several proteins. → 8.11

TipQ25. How do you prevent and correct batch effects in a large proteomics study?

Model answer. Batch = plate, reagent lot, run, day, instrument, operator. Prevent (design): randomize samples across plates, balance cases and controls, technical replicates across plates, common reference samples on every plate. Detect: boxplots by plate, PCA, clustering, controls, replicates. Correct (analysis): normalization across plates/batches (incl. reference-sample normalization), adjust for plate/run or include batch as a covariate. Also consider pre-analytical variation (fasting, time to centrifugation, freeze–thaw, hemolysis) that may differ systematically between cases and controls. → 8.6

TipQ26. Compare the three families of protein measurement technology.

Model answer. Antibody-based (ELISA, immunoassays, Western blot): targeted, quantitative with standards, clinical routine. Mass spectrometry: trypsin digestion → LC → electrospray → masses; detects isoforms, allows discovery; complex, dynamic-range problems, labelled peptides for calibration. High-throughput affinity (Olink, SomaScan): thousands of proteins in large cohorts (UKBB: ~50,000 people, ~3,000 proteins), relative abundance in NPX (log₂; 1 NPX = doubling). → 8.4–8.5
